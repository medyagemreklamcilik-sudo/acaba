Medieval people never bathed? Wrong. Very wrong.

I'm Berat, and this is the history they never taught you.

 In 1292, Paris alone had at least twenty-six public bathhouses. Every morning, criers walked the streets shouting that the water was hot. A steam bath cost two deniers. A full bath, four.

 London was... a bit different. Many bathhouses along the Thames were actually brothels. And guess who owned a lot of them? The Bishop of Winchester.

 Peasants couldn't go every day. They washed in rivers, in lakes, or with a basin and a jug. And washing your hands before and after meals? That was the rule for almost everyone.

Soap? They had it. Made at home from animal fat and wood ash. In the twelfth century, Spain started making olive oil soap, Castile soap. And from the East came soap from Aleppo.

 What about teeth? No toothbrush. They rubbed them with a rough linen cloth. One medieval medical text even has a recipe: sage and salt, baked until burnt, ground into powder, and rubbed on the teeth.

 Now, the toilet. In castles, toilets were tiny rooms sticking out of the wall. Everything dropped straight into the moat. Bodiam Castle in England had twenty-eight of them. Toilet paper? Moss, hay, leaves. If you were rich, wool or cloth.

 So where did the dirty Middle Ages myth come from? The really dirty part came after. In the fifteen hundreds, syphilis and plague closed the bathhouses one by one. Some doctors claimed warm water opened your pores and let disease in. People stopped washing, and started changing their shirts and wearing perfume instead.

 So the Middle Ages weren't the smelly ones.
